Speak Up: staff guide

A four-lesson self-advocacy course for TRACE students, ages 18 to 22. Students work through it on a Chromebook or phone, and each lesson ends with a one-page sheet for their folder. Open the course

The one rule

Some students know they have a disability. Some do not. The course never tells a student they have one, and neither should we while running it. Lessons 1, 3, and 4 do not use the word. Lesson 2 talks about “some people” and “a worker,” and the part about telling an employer is a section the student chooses to open.

If a student asks, “Do I have a disability?”, that conversation belongs to the case manager and the family. Say, “That is a good question for your case manager. Let’s ask together.”

How to run it

  • About 15 minutes a lesson. One or two lessons in a sitting is plenty. The order matters, but each lesson works on its own.
  • Print at the end of every lesson. Nothing is saved anywhere except that device, so the printed sheet is the record. The student writes their name by hand.
  • On a shared device, tap “Start fresh for a new student” on the home page after printing. It clears the last student’s answers.
  • The Listen button reads any screen out loud. Students who do not read well can still do the course, ideally with you beside them.
  • Lessons 2 to 4 open with two questions from earlier lessons. That is on purpose. Lessons may be days apart, and pulling the old material back is what makes it stick.
  • Students can type their own answers on any pick screen. Encourage it.

Your part: the out-loud practice

This is the step that matters most. Clicking through a web page does not change behavior on its own. Saying the words to a real person does.

  1. When the student prints, have them read “My words” to you out loud.
  2. Answer the way a supervisor or team member would. Keep it realistic and kind.
  3. Check the box on the sheet and initial it.
  4. Later, look for one real chance to use it: on a job site, on a community outing, or at their IEP meeting.

Resist answering for the student. Lesson 3 teaches them to say, “I would like to say it myself.” Let them.

Reading the sheet

  • Quiz, first try. Six scenario questions. Only the first tap counts, so the score is honest. The student still sees the best answer and why.
  • The skill column shows what each missed question was about. That is what to reteach.
  • Remembered from earlier lessons is the two-question recap score.
  • Before and after. Lesson 1 asks three confidence questions. Lesson 4 asks them again. If both were done on the same device, the Lesson 4 sheet shows them side by side. This is a quick snapshot, not a validated measure.

Things to know

  • Privacy. The page sends nothing anywhere and has no place to type a name. Do not add student names to the “own words” boxes.
  • Conservatorship. Lessons 2 and 4 tell students that school decisions became theirs at 18. For a student with a conservator who holds educational rights, that is not fully true. The lesson says so in one line and points them to the case manager. Know which students this applies to before they start Lesson 2.
  • Lesson 2 is information, not legal advice. If a student has a real problem at a job, bring in the case manager.
  • This is not an evidence-based curriculum. It borrows from ones that are. If you want the researched versions, look at the Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction, the Self-Directed IEP, and Whose Future Is It Anyway. The last two are free from the Zarrow Institute at the University of Oklahoma.
  • It needs reading or listening, and tapping. For students with more significant communication needs, a photo or video story the student builds about themselves is a better fit.
